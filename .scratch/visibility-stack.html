The pattern that repeats across all four: org/role → one mission sentence → specific, checkable history. None front-load a technology list; the stack shows up later, in the writing itself, not the summary.

C

Experience bullets & the confidentiality problem

The metric becomes the disclosure boundary: state the outcome precisely, keep the "how" generic enough not to leak IP.

The recurring formula from 2026 FDE/Applied-AI resume guidance (tryexponent.com):

[industry/client type, never named] + [constraint that signals seriousness] + [hard number]

Designed and shipped a production RAG system for a healthcare client in their VPC under HIPAA constraints, serving 50M documents with sub-300ms p95 retrieval latency.
example bullet pattern — tryexponent.com sourced

The client's name and the system's real architecture stay hidden; the constraint (HIPAA, VPC, Fortune 500 scale) and the number do the credibility work instead. Career-advice consensus on NDA-bound work: job title, employer, and public-facing outcome are fair game; internal tool names, model names, and proprietary architecture are not (resumeworded.com).

Company-specific framing of the same role

Sierra reframes its FDEs as "Agent Engineers" who "own the full Agent Development Life Cycle end-to-end" — the renamed title claims full-stack, customer-facing scope rather than a narrow integration label (tryexponent.com/jobs/fde/sierra). Harvey's FDEs embed inside BigLaw firms for 6–9 months; the public description stays at the workflow/vertical/cadence level, never the prompt or eval pipeline underneath (getperspective.ai).

Research-track resumes flip the ratio

For MTS/research roles, the advice inverts: "If you have done interesting independent research, written an insightful blog post, or made substantial contributions to open-source software, put that at the top" (simplify.jobs). Metrics-over-narrative for deployment roles, narrative-over-metrics for research roles — the split tracks the role, not the company.

D

What actually makes someone visible

The mechanism, by subfield — and it's narrower than "write a blog and open-source something."

MCP: a credibility ladder with a top rung

The ladder is legible now that MCP was donated to the Agentic AI Foundation (Linux Foundation) in Dec 2025 — a named-maintainer slot there reads like a CNCF TOC seat (maintainer-update post). MCP's creators, David Soria Parra and Justin Spahr-Summers, built visibility through two channels: the a16z podcast and repeat Latent Space appearances, including the "One Year of MCP" retrospective.

Below that tier, the actual mechanism: ship a narrow server solving one annoying problem → get pulled into a curated "awesome" list → get cited by a name-brand analyst. idosal's git-mcp ("put an end to code hallucinations") went from personal repo to sitting alongside official servers in mcp-servers-hub. Company-branded pairs — github-mcp-server, postman-mcp-server (with launch post), Hasura's promptql-mcp — show the pattern: a named engineer's commit history plus a company blog post is what recruiters actually search on. Simon Willison is the single most consistent amplifier in this niche — a link from his weblog (e.g. on stateless MCP) functions as MCP's version of a Hacker News front page.

Evals: course + reference doc, not repo stars alone

Hamel Husain and Shreya Shankar's "AI Evals for Engineers & PMs" course has run 4,500+ engineers from OpenAI, Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft through it, backed by the long-running Evals FAQ and a Lenny's Newsletter feature. Eugene Yan's essay "Task-Specific LLM Evals that Do & Don't Work" and the multi-author "What We Learned from a Year of Building with LLMs" (with Bryan Bischof, Shreya Shankar, Charles Frye, Jason Liu, Hamel Husain) is probably the most-cited applied-AI artifact of the past two years — every co-author benefited from the shared byline. On the repo side, Jeffrey Ip's DeepEval proves stars still count on their own: 17.4k stars, a pytest-style API, plus a steady internals-post cadence like "How I Built Deterministic LLM Evaluation Metrics." Braintrust does the same move at company scale with its open "Behavior Specs" standard.

Memory: thinner air, company blogs over personal brands

Mem0's own "State of AI Agent Memory 2026" report is the clean example of a repeatable move: publish a benchmark that positions your own product as the reference point. Zep (getzep/zep) plays the adoption-proof game with multi-language SDKs. Neither subfield has a Willison- or Husain-level individual figurehead yet — credibility here currently accrues to company blogs, not personal ones.

The mechanism, synthesized

Artifact → recruiter inbound runs through four chokepoints, and going through zero of them is why most solid work stays invisible: (1) inclusion in an official or heavily-starred curated list, (2) a Willison/Latent-Space/Lenny's-style amplifier linking it, (3) a company blog post pairing the repo with a named engineer's byline, (4) a benchmark or "state of X" report that makes the author the category's reference point.

India vs. US/remote

Visibility for India-based engineers runs more through LinkedIn and founder-centric press than X threads — Krutrim's coverage (TechCrunch) tracks founder Bhavish Aggarwal's persona more than any repo; Sarvam AI's credibility leans on a benchmark claim (84.3% on olmOCR-Bench) amplified through Indian tech press, not a viral OSS launch. The India-specific analog to a Latent Space podcast slot is the meetup circuit: AI Tinkerers Bangalore, Anthropic's Builder Summit in Bengaluru, the Agentic AI Community Bengaluru meetup — in-person demo credibility over X-native build-in-public virality. Enterprise B2B names (Postman, Hasura, Atlan) publish through company engineering blogs regardless of the engineer's location, which reads as the more reliable path for India-based contributors right now.

What separates "helps" from "ignored"

  1. Frequency + specificity beats a static project grid. A portfolio last touched two years ago with three cards reads — and ranks — as abandoned.
  2. The writing is the demo. None of these sites lean on an embedded chat widget as the primary artifact; the post itself (code, methodology, named users) is the proof.
  3. Reference docs beat resumes. applied-llms.org and applyingml.com outperform any About page because other people link to them as infrastructure — compounding backlinks a static bio never gets.
  4. One high-leverage essay can outperform years of a resume site — swyx's and Liu's career inflection points both trace to a single post, not a redesign.
  5. Verifiable specifics beat adjectives. Named clients, named course enrollees, named prior-employer projects — every site above leads with something checkable, never "passionate about AI."
Caveat. Post counts and enrollment numbers above are self-reported on each site, not independently re-verified — treat as directional, not audited.
F

Member of Technical Staff, and title-as-signal

MTS traces to Bell Labs, where it read as fellowship status, not corporate rank.

OpenAI revived it in 2023 — Greg Brockman has credited Alan Kay's Xerox PARC culture — and Anthropic made it the standard designation company-wide; the Claude Code team reportedly runs "5 roles and zero job titles." The stated logic, per simplify.jobs: "With no level attached, people can work across pre-training, post-training, product, and infra without renegotiating their title every time." Independently corroborated as a soft anti-poaching mechanism — uniform titling makes it harder for rivals to target seniority via LinkedIn (trevorlasn.com).

What this means for someone outside the lab

A traditional ladder ("Senior/Staff/Principal Engineer") signals scope and comp band on sight. MTS signals almost nothing externally — so candidates must front-load other proof: public research, OSS contributions, shipped demos, since the title itself won't do the talking. Startups lean the opposite direction on purpose: Founding Engineer, Applied AI Engineer, Agent Engineer are deliberately more descriptive than MTS, because a startup's title has to do the credibility work a lab's brand already does (coffeespace.com, lyzr.ai). Expect any prior functional title (data scientist, infra engineer) to flatten to MTS on hire at a lab — so the LinkedIn story has to carry seniority signals the title no longer will.

G

Closing the gap — for 1.7 years, MCP servers, agent memory, Langfuse evals, KG RAG

Mapped against the four chokepoints in D: the underlying work already qualifies. What's missing is which of the four mechanisms it's been pushed through — right now, probably none.

1

Publish one MCP server as a standalone, generalized repo

Strip the enterprise-specific parts, ship it solving one narrow problem well, submit it to mcp-servers-hub or an awesome-mcp-servers list. This is the git-mcp path — a personal repo that earned a seat next to official servers.

2

Write the Evals-FAQ-style post on Langfuse methodology

Narrow and opinionated beats broad: one sharp post on a real eval failure mode and how it was measured and fixed, in Hamel Husain's register — not a Langfuse feature tour.

3

Turn the KG-RAG + agent-memory work into a "state of X" comparison

Memory is the thinnest subfield (section D) — a benchmark-style post comparing graph-based memory against Mem0/Zep-style approaches has real odds of becoming a cited reference point, the way Mem0's own report did for itself.

4

Get one link from an actual amplifier

Submit the MCP server or the eval post where a Willison-equivalent could see it, or — India-specific — demo it live at AI Tinkerers Bangalore or an Anthropic Builder Summit. One credible amplifier link outperforms months of unlinked posting.

5

Rebuild the portfolio as a writing archive, not a project grid

Per section E: lead with a Writing section, publish on a real cadence, and let one long, technical post (the eval or memory piece) be the primary artifact — not a demo widget.

6

Rewrite the LinkedIn headline and About to the confirmed pattern

Org/role → one mission sentence → specific, checkable history (section B). Drop adjectives; put the OSS/research artifact from #1–#3 at the top, per the MTS resume advice in section C.

7

Reframe experience bullets with the FDE formula

[industry, unnamed] + [constraint that signals seriousness] + [hard number] — see section C. Keep internal system names out; keep the metric and the constraint in.
